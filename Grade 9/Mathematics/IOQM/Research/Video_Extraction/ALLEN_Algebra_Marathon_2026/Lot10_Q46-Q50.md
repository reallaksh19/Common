## Q46. Integer roots force an arithmetic progression

### Question

Let \(N\) be the number of triples of positive integers \((a,b,c)\) with

\[
a\le b\le c\le100
\]

such that

\[
P(x)
=
x^2+\left(a^2+4b^2+c^2+1\right)x
+\left(4ab+4bc-2ca\right)
\]

has integer roots.

Find the last three digits of \(N\).

**Answer:** \(\boxed{550}\)

**Original source identified:** 2016 CMIMC Team Round, Problem 8.

### Short solution

Set

\[
A=a^2+4b^2+c^2+1,\qquad
B=4ab+4bc-2ca.
\]

Because \(a\le b\le c\),

\[
B=4ab+2c(2b-a)>0.
\]

If \(P\) has integer roots, their sum is \(-A<0\) and product is \(B>0\), so both roots are negative integers. Write them as \(-u,-v\) with \(u,v\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\). Then

\[
P(-1)=(u-1)(v-1)\ge0.
\]

But directly,

\[
P(-1)
=
1-A+B
=
-(a-2b+c)^2
\le0.
\]

Therefore

\[
a-2b+c=0,
\]

so

\[
a+c=2b.
\]

Conversely, if \(a+c=2b\), then \(P(-1)=0\). Since \(P\) is monic with integer coefficients, the other root is also an integer. Thus the condition is **equivalent** to

\[
a+c=2b.
\]

So we count positive three-term arithmetic progressions

\[
a\le b\le c\le100.
\]

For fixed \(b\),

\[
c=2b-a\le100
\]

requires

\[
\max(1,2b-100)\le a\le b.
\]

Hence

\[
N
=
\sum_{b=1}^{50}b
+
\sum_{b=51}^{100}(101-b)
=
1275+1275
=
2550.
\]

The last three digits are

\[
\boxed{550}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For a monic quadratic with integer roots, evaluate it at a strategically chosen integer.

Here the identity

\[
P(-1)=-(a-2b+c)^2
\]

is decisive. Since both roots must be negative integers, \(P(-1)\) is also a product of two nonnegative integers. The only way it can simultaneously be \(\ge0\) and \(\le0\) is to vanish.

This converts an “integer roots” condition into the simple AP condition

\[
a+c=2b.
\]

**Necessary and sufficient condition:** Independently proved.  
**Count:** \(2550\), hence last three digits \(550\).

## Q47. Product of an arithmetic and a geometric sequence

### Question

Let

\[
a_1,a_2,\ldots
\]

be an arithmetic sequence and

\[
b_1,b_2,\ldots
\]

be a geometric sequence.

Suppose

\[
a_1b_1=20,\qquad
a_2b_2=19,\qquad
a_3b_3=14.
\]

Find the greatest possible value of

\[
a_4b_4.
\]

**Answer:**

\[
\boxed{\frac{37}{4}}.
\]

**Original source identified:** HMMT February 2019, Algebra & Number Theory, Problem 5.

### Short solution

Let

\[
a_1=A,\qquad a_n=A+(n-1)d,
\]

and let the geometric ratio of \(b_n\) be \(r\). Put

\[
b_1=B.
\]

Then

\[
AB=20.
\]

From

\[
(A+d)Br=19,
\]

we get

\[
Bd=\frac{19-20r}{r}.
\]

Also

\[
(A+2d)Br^2=14.
\]

Substituting \(AB=20\),

\[
(20+2Bd)r^2=14.
\]

Using the expression for \(Bd\),

\[
10r^2-19r+7=0,
\]

so

\[
(5r-7)(2r-1)=0.
\]

Hence

\[
r=\frac75
\quad\text{or}\quad
r=\frac12.
\]

Now

\[
a_4b_4
=
(A+3d)Br^3
=
(20+3Bd)r^3
=
r^2(57-40r).
\]

For \(r=\frac75\),

\[
a_4b_4=\frac{49}{25}.
\]

For \(r=\frac12\),

\[
a_4b_4=\frac{37}{4}.
\]

Therefore the greatest possible value is

\[
\boxed{\frac{37}{4}}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

When the product of an AP term and a GP term is specified, do **not** try to reconstruct both sequences separately.

Write

\[
a_n=A+(n-1)d,\qquad
b_n=Br^{n-1},
\]

and work with the combined quantities \(AB\) and \(Bd\). This reduces the system to a low-degree equation for the GP ratio \(r\).

### Verification status

**Problem and answer:** Fully verified from the official HMMT solution.  
**All algebraic ratios:** Checked.  
**Maximum:** \(\frac{37}{4}\).  
**Video numbering:** **Level D — not directly video-confirmed.**

## Q48. Digit sums divisible by \(11\)

### Question

A positive integer is called **detestable** if the sum of its digits is a multiple of \(11\).

How many positive integers below \(10000\) are detestable?

**Answer:** \(\boxed{908}\)

**Original source identified:** 2023 CMIMC Team Round, Problem 11.

### Short solution

Write every integer from \(1\) to \(9999\) as a four-digit string

\[
\overline{abcd}
\]

allowing leading zeros.

The digit sum lies between \(1\) and \(36\), so the possible positive multiples of \(11\) are

\[
11,\quad22,\quad33.
\]

We count digit quadruples \(0\le a,b,c,d\le9\).

### Sum \(11\)

Without the upper bound \(9\),

\[
\binom{14}{3}=364.
\]

If one digit is at least \(10\), subtract \(10\); there are

\[
4\binom43=16
\]

such solutions. Thus

\[
364-16=348.
\]

### Sum \(22\)

Unrestricted:

\[
\binom{25}{3}=2300.
\]

Subtract cases with one digit at least \(10\):

\[
4\binom{15}{3}=1820.
\]

Add back cases with two digits at least \(10\):

\[
\binom42\binom53=60.
\]

Hence

\[
2300-1820+60=540.
\]

### Sum \(33\)

Replace each digit \(d_i\) by \(9-d_i\). Then the complementary digit sum is

\[
36-33=3.
\]

So the number of solutions is

\[
\binom63=20.
\]

Therefore the total number is

\[
348+540+20=\boxed{908}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For bounded digit-sum problems, represent shorter numbers with leading zeros and use stars-and-bars plus inclusion-exclusion.

For sums near the maximum digit sum, the complement transformation

\[
d_i\mapsto9-d_i
\]

can make the count much easier.

### Verification status

**Problem and answer:** Fully verified from the published CMIMC source.  
**Digit bounds:** Explicitly enforced.  
**Zero excluded:** Yes; its digit sum is \(0\), but the problem asks for positive integers and the counted sums are \(11,22,33\).

## Q49. AP followed by GP

### Question

In an increasing sequence of four positive integers, the first three terms form an arithmetic progression, the last three terms form a geometric progression, and the first and fourth terms differ by \(30\).

Find the sum of the four terms.

**Answer:** \(\boxed{129}\)

**Original source identified:** 2003 AIME I, Problem 8.

> **Worksheet duplication note:** This is the same mathematical problem that appears earlier as worksheet Q37. It is genuinely repeated in the associated Algebra sheet.

### Short solution

Write the first three terms as

\[
a-d,\qquad a,\qquad a+d,
\]

where \(d>0\).

Since the last three form a GP,

\[
a\cdot T_4=(a+d)^2,
\]

so

\[
T_4=\frac{(a+d)^2}{a}.
\]

The first and fourth differ by \(30\):

\[
\frac{(a+d)^2}{a}-(a-d)=30.
\]

This simplifies to

\[
3d+\frac{d^2}{a}=30,
\]

hence

\[
a=\frac{d^2}{3(10-d)}.
\]

Thus \(d<10\). Integrality forces \(3\mid d\), leaving

\[
d=3,6,9.
\]

Checking positivity and integrality:

- \(d=3\): \(a\) is not an integer;
- \(d=6\): the first term is nonpositive;
- \(d=9\): \(a=27\).

Hence the sequence is

\[
18,\ 27,\ 36,\ 48.
\]

Its sum is

\[
18+27+36+48=\boxed{129}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For three terms in AP, use the symmetric form

\[
a-d,\ a,\ a+d.
\]

Then impose the GP condition by

\[
(\text{middle})\times(\text{fourth})=(\text{third})^2.
\]

Positivity and integrality usually reduce the final parameter check to a handful of cases.

## Q50. Alternating GP/AP recurrence

### Question

A sequence of positive integers satisfies

\[
a_1=1
\]

and

\[
a_9+a_{10}=646.
\]

For every \(n\ge1\),

\[
a_{2n-1},a_{2n},a_{2n+1}
\]

are in geometric progression, while

\[
a_{2n},a_{2n+1},a_{2n+2}
\]

are in arithmetic progression.

Let \(a_N\) be the greatest term of the sequence that is less than \(1000\).

Find

\[
N+a_N.
\]

**Answer:** \(\boxed{973}\)

**Original source identified:** 2004 AIME II, Problem 9.

> **Corrected reconstruction:** The worksheet text extraction corrupts the general recurrence and contains an extra malformed phrase. The statement above is restored from the published 2004 AIME II problem.

### Short solution

Because

\[
a_1,a_2,a_3
\]

are in GP and \(a_1=1\), write

\[
a_2=r,\qquad a_3=r^2.
\]

Since all terms are positive integers, \(r\) is a positive integer.

The AP condition gives

\[
a_4=2r^2-r=r(2r-1).
\]

Then the next GP gives

\[
a_5=\frac{a_4^2}{a_3}=(2r-1)^2.
\]

Continuing alternately,

\[
a_6=(2r-1)(3r-2),
\]

\[
a_7=(3r-2)^2,
\]

\[
a_8=(3r-2)(4r-3),
\]

\[
a_9=(4r-3)^2,
\]

\[
a_{10}=(4r-3)(5r-4).
\]

Thus

\[
a_9+a_{10}
=
(4r-3)(9r-7)
=
646.
\]

So

\[
36r^2-55r-625=0.
\]

Its roots are

\[
r=5
\quad\text{and}\quad
r=-\frac{125}{36}.
\]

Since \(r>0\),

\[
r=5.
\]

The terms now follow

\[
a_{2k-1}=(4k-3)^2,
\]

\[
a_{2k}=(4k-3)(4k+1).
\]

Near \(1000\),

\[
a_{15}=29^2=841,
\]

\[
a_{16}=29\cdot33=957,
\]

\[
a_{17}=33^2=1089.
\]

Hence the greatest term below \(1000\) is

\[
a_{16}=957.
\]

Therefore

\[
N+a_N
=
16+957
=
\boxed{973}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

When GP and AP conditions alternate, compute the first few terms symbolically before searching for a closed form.

Here the factors evolve linearly:

\[
1,\ r,\ 2r-1,\ 3r-2,\ 4r-3,\ldots
\]

and the terms become alternating squares and adjacent products. Once the pattern is visible, prove it by the two recurrence rules and use it directly.
