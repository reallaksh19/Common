## Worksheet candidate 1 — Symmetric system in two positive integers

### Question

Find \(x^2+y^2\) if \(x\) and \(y\) are positive integers satisfying

\[
xy+x+y=71
\]

and

\[
x^2y+xy^2=880.
\]

**Answer:** \(\boxed{146}\)

**Original source identified:** 1991 AIME, Problem 1.

### Short solution

Let

\[
s=x+y,\qquad p=xy.
\]

Then

\[
p+s=71,
\]

while

\[
x^2y+xy^2=xy(x+y)=ps=880.
\]

Thus \(s\) and \(p\) are the two roots of

\[
t^2-71t+880=0=(t-16)(t-55),
\]

so \(\{s,p\}=\{16,55\}\).

If \(s=55,p=16\), positive integers \(x,y\) with product \(16\) cannot have sum \(55\). Hence

\[
x+y=16,\qquad xy=55,
\]

giving \(\{x,y\}=\{5,11\}\).

Therefore

\[
x^2+y^2=(x+y)^2-2xy=16^2-2(55)=146.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

When a two-variable system is symmetric in \(x,y\), immediately test

\[
s=x+y,\qquad p=xy.
\]

Expressions such as

\[
x^2y+xy^2=xy(x+y)
\]

then become \(ps\), often reducing the problem to a quadratic in \(s,p\).

## Worksheet candidate 2 — Cyclic reciprocal system

### Question

Suppose \(x,y,z\) are positive numbers satisfying

\[
xyz=1,\qquad x+\frac1z=5,\qquad y+\frac1x=29.
\]

If

\[
z+\frac1y=\frac mn
\]

where \(m,n\) are relatively prime positive integers, find \(m+n\).

**Answer:** \(\boxed{5}\)

**Original source identified:** 2000 AIME I, Problem 7.

### Short solution

Set

\[
t=z+\frac1y.
\]

Multiply the three cyclic expressions:

\[
\left(x+\frac1z\right)
\left(y+\frac1x\right)
\left(z+\frac1y\right).
\]

Using \(xyz=1\), expansion simplifies to

\[
xyz+\frac1{xyz}
+\left(x+\frac1z\right)
+\left(y+\frac1x\right)
+\left(z+\frac1y\right).
\]

Hence

\[
5\cdot29\cdot t=1+1+5+29+t,
\]

so

\[
145t=36+t.
\]

Thus

\[
144t=36,\qquad t=\frac14.
\]

Therefore

\[
m+n=1+4=\boxed5.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For a cyclic system with \(xyz=1\), try multiplying expressions of the form

\[
x+\frac1z,\qquad y+\frac1x,\qquad z+\frac1y.
\]

The eight-term product can collapse because reciprocal products become \(1/(xyz)=1\), while the remaining terms regroup into the original cyclic sums.

## Worksheet candidate 3 — Exponential equation and Vieta

### Question

The equation

\[
2^{333x-2}+2^{111x+2}=2^{222x+1}+1
\]

has three real roots. Their sum is \(\frac mn\), where \(m,n\) are relatively prime positive integers. Find \(m+n\).

**Answer:** \(\boxed{113}\)

**Original source identified:** 2005 AIME I, Problem 8.

> **Notation correction:** The third-party text extraction of the ALLEN sheet flattens the exponents. The displayed equation above is restored from the independently identified published AIME statement.

### Short solution

Let

\[
u=2^{111x}>0.
\]

Then

\[
2^{333x-2}=\frac{u^3}{4},\qquad
2^{111x+2}=4u,\qquad
2^{222x+1}=2u^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{u^3}{4}+4u=2u^2+1,
\]

or

\[
u^3-8u^2+16u-4=0.
\]

Let its three positive roots be \(u_1,u_2,u_3\). By Vieta,

\[
u_1u_2u_3=4.
\]

If the corresponding original roots are \(x_1,x_2,x_3\), then

\[
u_i=2^{111x_i}.
\]

Hence

\[
2^{111(x_1+x_2+x_3)}
=u_1u_2u_3
=4=2^2.
\]

Thus

\[
x_1+x_2+x_3=\frac{2}{111}.
\]

So

\[
m+n=2+111=\boxed{113}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For exponential equations whose exponents are integer multiples of one basic exponent, substitute

\[
u=a^{kx}.
\]

If the target is the **sum of the original \(x\)-roots**, the useful Vieta datum may be the **product** of the transformed \(u\)-roots:

\[
u_1u_2\cdots u_r
=a^{k(x_1+\cdots+x_r)}.
\]

## Worksheet candidate 4 — Averages after deleting extremes

### Question

Suppose \(S\) is a finite set of positive integers.

- If the greatest integer in \(S\) is removed, the average of the remaining integers is \(32\).
- If the least integer is then also removed, the average is \(35\).
- If the greatest integer is returned, the average rises to \(40\).
- The greatest integer in the original set is \(72\) greater than the least integer.

What is the average of all the integers in \(S\)?

**Answer:** \(\boxed{36.8}\)

**Original source identified:** 2021 AMC 10B, Problem 19; also 2021 AMC 12B, Problem 12.

### Short solution

Let

\[
n=|S|,\qquad T=\sum_{s\in S}s,
\]

and let \(M,L\) be the greatest and least elements.

The first and third conditions give

\[
T-M=32(n-1),
\]

and

\[
T-L=40(n-1).
\]

Subtract:

\[
M-L=8(n-1).
\]

Since \(M-L=72\),

\[
72=8(n-1),
\]

so

\[
n=10.
\]

Removing both extremes leaves \(8\) numbers with total

\[
35\cdot8=280.
\]

Removing only the least leaves \(9\) numbers with total

\[
40\cdot9=360,
\]

so

\[
M=360-280=80.
\]

Then

\[
L=80-72=8,
\]

and

\[
T=280+80+8=368.
\]

Therefore the original average is

\[
\frac{368}{10}=\boxed{36.8}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

Translate every average condition into a **sum equation**. If two modified sets have the same size, subtracting their totals often isolates exactly the deleted/reinserted elements. Here,

\[
(T-L)-(T-M)=M-L
\]

immediately determines the set size.

## Worksheet candidate 5 — Arithmetic progression of polygon angles

### Question

The degree measures of the angles in a convex \(18\)-sided polygon form an increasing arithmetic sequence with integer values. Find the degree measure of the smallest angle.

**Answer:** \(\boxed{143^\circ}\)

**Original source identified:** 2011 AIME II, Problem 3.

### Short solution

The sum of the interior angles is

\[
(18-2)180^\circ=2880^\circ.
\]

Let the smallest angle be \(a\) and the positive integer common difference be \(d\). Then

\[
\frac{18}{2}(2a+17d)=2880,
\]

so

\[
2a+17d=320.
\]

Because \(2a\) and \(320\) are even, \(17d\) is even, hence \(d\) is even. Thus

\[
d\ge2.
\]

The largest angle is

\[
a+17d=\frac{320+17d}{2}.
\]

Convexity requires it to be less than \(180^\circ\):

\[
\frac{320+17d}{2}<180,
\]

so

\[
17d<40,
\]

hence \(d\le2\).

Therefore

\[
d=2,
\]

and

\[
a=\frac{320-34}{2}=143.
\]

Thus the smallest angle is

\[
\boxed{143^\circ}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

For polygon-angle arithmetic progressions, combine three constraints:

1. polygon interior-angle sum;
2. integrality/parity of the common difference;
3. convexity, which forces every interior angle to be \(<180^\circ\).

The convexity bound often collapses several arithmetic possibilities to one.
