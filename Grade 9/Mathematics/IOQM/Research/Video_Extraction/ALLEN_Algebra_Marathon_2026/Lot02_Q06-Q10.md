## Worksheet candidate 6 — Integer factorization

### Question

Find \(3x^2y^2\) if \(x\) and \(y\) are integers such that

\[
y^2+3x^2y^2=30x^2+517.
\]

**Answer:** \(\boxed{588}\)

**Original source identified:** 1987 AIME, Problem 5.

### Short solution

Rearrange by completing a useful product:

\[
3x^2y^2+y^2-30x^2=517.
\]

Then

\[
(3x^2+1)(y^2-10)=507.
\]

Since \(3x^2+1>0\), both factors are positive divisors of

\[
507=3\cdot13^2.
\]

Also,

\[
3x^2+1\equiv1\pmod 3.
\]

The viable divisor is

\[
3x^2+1=13,
\]

which gives

\[
x^2=4.
\]

Then

\[
y^2-10=\frac{507}{13}=39,
\]

so

\[
y^2=49.
\]

Therefore

\[
3x^2y^2=3\cdot4\cdot49=\boxed{588}.
\]

For completeness, the other divisor possibilities congruent to \(1\pmod3\) do not give simultaneous integer squares.

### IOQM tip / trick

When an equation mixes \(x^2y^2,x^2,y^2\), try **Simon’s Favorite Factoring Trick**:

\[
(AX+B)(CY+D).
\]

Here the strategically added constant gives

\[
(3x^2+1)(y^2-10)=507,
\]

turning a Diophantine equation into a small divisor check.

## Worksheet candidate 7 — Two discriminant constraints

### Question

For how many ordered pairs \((b,c)\) of positive integers does neither

\[
x^2+bx+c=0
\]

nor

\[
x^2+cx+b=0
\]

have two distinct real solutions?

**Answer:** \(\boxed{6}\)

**Original source identified:** 2021 Fall AMC 10A, Problem 20; also 2021 Fall AMC 12A, Problem 17.

### Short solution

A quadratic fails to have two distinct real roots exactly when its discriminant is nonpositive.

Thus

\[
b^2-4c\le0
\]

and

\[
c^2-4b\le0.
\]

So

\[
b^2\le4c,\qquad c^2\le4b.
\]

Square the first inequality:

\[
b^4\le16c^2.
\]

Using \(c^2\le4b\),

\[
b^4\le64b.
\]

Because \(b>0\),

\[
b^3\le64,
\]

hence

\[
b\le4.
\]

Now enumerate:

- \(b=1\): \(c=1,2\);
- \(b=2\): \(c=1,2\);
- \(b=3\): \(c=3\);
- \(b=4\): \(c=4\).

Therefore there are

\[
2+2+1+1=\boxed6
\]

ordered pairs.

### IOQM tip / trick

Translate statements about the **number of real roots** immediately into discriminant inequalities.

If two inequalities constrain each variable in terms of the other, chain them to obtain a finite bound before doing any casework:

\[
b^2\le4c,\quad c^2\le4b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
b^4\le64b.
\]

## Worksheet candidate 8 — Maximizing a difference from symmetric data

### Question

Real numbers \(x\) and \(y\) satisfy

\[
x^2+y^2=2023
\]

and

\[
(x-2)(y-2)=3.
\]

Find the largest possible value of

\[
|x-y|.
\]

**Correct answer:** \(\boxed{\sqrt{2197}}\)

**Original source identified:** 2023 Carnegie Mellon Informatics and Mathematics Competition (CMIMC), Team Round, Problem 2.

> **Corrected reconstruction / answer-key correction:** The ALLEN worksheet statement itself asks for \(|x-y|\), but its printed answer key gives \(2197\). The original CMIMC source gives \(\sqrt{2197}\), and independent calculation confirms this. The worksheet key appears to have reported the maximum value of \((x-y)^2\) instead.

### Short solution

Let

\[
s=x+y,\qquad p=xy.
\]

From

\[
(x-2)(y-2)=3
\]

we get

\[
p-2s+4=3,
\]

hence

\[
p=2s-1.
\]

Also,

\[
x^2+y^2=s^2-2p=2023.
\]

Substitute \(p=2s-1\):

\[
s^2-4s+2=2023,
\]

so

\[
s^2-4s-2021=0.
\]

Thus

\[
s=47\quad\text{or}\quad s=-43.
\]

Now

\[
(x-y)^2=s^2-4p=s^2-4(2s-1).
\]

For \(s=47\),

\[
(x-y)^2=1837.
\]

For \(s=-43\),

\[
(x-y)^2=2197.
\]

Hence

\[
\max |x-y|=\boxed{\sqrt{2197}}.
\]

Both cases are admissible because the corresponding quadratic equations for \(x,y\) have positive discriminant.

### IOQM tip / trick

For conditions symmetric in \(x,y\), use

\[
s=x+y,\qquad p=xy.
\]

Then remember the key identity

\[
(x-y)^2=s^2-4p.
\]

When maximizing an absolute difference, it is often easier to maximize its square first — but **take the square root at the end**.

## Worksheet candidate 9 — Symmetric expressions in two complex numbers

### Question

Let \(a\) and \(b\) be complex numbers such that

\[
(a+1)(b+1)=2
\]

and

\[
(a^2+1)(b^2+1)=32.
\]

Compute the sum of all possible values of

\[
(a^4+1)(b^4+1).
\]

**Answer:** \(\boxed{1160}\)

**Original source identified:** 2021 CMIMC, Algebra & Number Theory Division 2, Problem 6.

### Short solution

Let

\[
s=a+b,\qquad p=ab.
\]

The first equation gives

\[
p+s+1=2,
\]

so

\[
s=1-p.
\]

The second equation is

\[
p^2+(a^2+b^2)+1=32.
\]

Since

\[
a^2+b^2=s^2-2p,
\]

we get

\[
p^2+(1-p)^2-2p+1=32.
\]

Thus

\[
p^2-2p-15=0,
\]

so

\[
p=5\quad\text{or}\quad p=-3.
\]

For the target,

\[
(a^4+1)(b^4+1)
=p^4+(a^4+b^4)+1,
\]

and

\[
a^4+b^4=(a^2+b^2)^2-2p^2.
\]

### Case 1: \(p=5\)

Then \(s=-4\), so

\[
a^2+b^2=s^2-2p=6.
\]

Hence

\[
(a^4+1)(b^4+1)
=5^4+6^2-2(5^2)+1
=612.
\]

### Case 2: \(p=-3\)

Then \(s=4\), so

\[
a^2+b^2=16+6=22.
\]

Hence

\[
(a^4+1)(b^4+1)
=(-3)^4+22^2-2(9)+1
=548.
\]

Therefore the sum of all possible values is

\[
612+548=\boxed{1160}.
\]

Both cases correspond to legitimate complex pairs \(a,b\), so neither value may be discarded.

### IOQM tip / trick

When the problem is symmetric in two variables, replace the variables by

\[
s=a+b,\qquad p=ab.
\]

Higher power sums can then be generated without solving for \(a,b\):

\[
a^2+b^2=s^2-2p,
\]

\[
a^4+b^4=(a^2+b^2)^2-2p^2.
\]

This is usually much cleaner than finding the individual complex roots.

## Worksheet candidate 10 — Common-value substitution

### Question

Suppose \(a,b,c\) are nonzero real numbers such that

\[
bc+\frac1a
=
ca+\frac2b
=
ab+\frac7c
=
\frac1{a+b+c}.
\]

Find

\[
a+b+c.
\]

**Answer:**

\[
\boxed{-\frac{\sqrt[3]{3}}2}.
\]

**Original source identified:** 2018 Carnegie Mellon Informatics and Mathematics Competition (CMIMC), Algebra Round, Problem 5.

### Short solution

Let the common value be \(k\). Then

\[
bc+\frac1a=k,\qquad
ca+\frac2b=k,\qquad
ab+\frac7c=k.
\]

Multiplying respectively by \(a,b,c\),

\[
abc+1=ak,
\]

\[
abc+2=bk,
\]

\[
abc+7=ck.
\]

Adding,

\[
3abc+10=(a+b+c)k.
\]

But

\[
k=\frac1{a+b+c},
\]

so

\[
(a+b+c)k=1.
\]

Therefore

\[
3abc+10=1,
\]

hence

\[
abc=-3.
\]

Substituting back,

\[
ak=-2,\qquad bk=-1,\qquad ck=4.
\]

Multiply these three equations:

\[
abc\,k^3=8.
\]

Using \(abc=-3\),

\[
k^3=-\frac83,
\]

so

\[
k=-\frac2{\sqrt[3]{3}}.
\]

Finally,

\[
a+b+c=\frac1k
=-\frac{\sqrt[3]{3}}2.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{a+b+c=-\frac{\sqrt[3]{3}}2}.
\]

### IOQM tip / trick

When several expressions are equal, introduce a common value:

\[
E_1=E_2=E_3=E_4=k.
\]

Then multiply equations by denominators to create related expressions. Here this produces

\[
abc+1,\quad abc+2,\quad abc+7,
\]

whose sum interacts perfectly with

\[
k(a+b+c)=1.
\]

This “common-value substitution” often turns a messy rational system into linear relations.
